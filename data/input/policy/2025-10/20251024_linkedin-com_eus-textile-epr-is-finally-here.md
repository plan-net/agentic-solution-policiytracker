The European Union has officially adopted Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) for textiles, and if you're in fashion, retail, or e-commerce, IMO it's a much needed key shift in who's responsible for textile waste.

In September 2025, the European Parliament gave final approval to new measures requiring producers that make textiles available in the EU to cover the costs of collection, sorting, and recycling through Extended Producer Responsibility schemes to be set up by each member state within 30 months of the directive's entry into force.

This applies to clothing and accessories, hats, footwear, blankets, bed and kitchen linen, and curtains. Member states may also set up EPR schemes for mattress producers.

The really key bit? These provisions apply to all producers, including those using e-commerce tools and irrespective of whether they are established in an EU country or outside the Union. So if you're selling textiles into the EU from anywhere in the world, this applies to you!

### Polluter Pays Principle

Here's how it works: the more wasteful or difficult to recycle your products are, the higher fees you'll pay. Simple.

Financial contributions will be "eco-modulated" based on the product's environmental performance: producers will pay more for products that are harder to recycle or have a bigger environmental footprint, and less for more sustainable designs.

IMO this is the polluter pays principle finally applied to fashion, and it's about time. For years, the burden of dealing with textile waste has fallen on local authorities and waste management systems (and the guilt to the users!). Now it's where it should be, with the producers who design and sell the products in the first place, tackling the problem at source (not after the fact)

### So What (Why This Matters)?

Let's look at the scale of the problem we're dealing with. Every year, 12.6 million tonnes of textile waste are generated in the EU, with clothing and footwear alone accounting for 5.2 million tonnes of waste, equivalent to 12 kg of waste per person every year.

> It is estimated that less than 1% of all textiles worldwide are recycled into new products.

This regulation creates direct financial incentives for better design from the start. Durability, repairability, and recyclability aren't just nice-to-haves anymore BUT NOW directly impact your bottom line. It also funds the infrastructure we need to keep textiles in circulation rather than landfill.

Member states should also address ultra-fast fashion and fast fashion practices when deciding on financial contributions to the EPR schemes. Translation: if your business model is built on cheap, disposable clothing, expect higher fees.

The legislation will enter into force 20 days after publication in the EU Official Journal, and member states will then have 20 months to adopt national legislation implementing the new requirements.

EPR schemes for textiles must be established by each member state within 30 months of the revised Directive's entry into force, meaning that producers could face cost and reporting obligations as early as 2028, depending on the speed of national implementation.

Micro-enterprises get an extra year to comply, but everyone else needs to start preparing now.

Meanwhile, all EU member states must establish separate textile waste collection systems by January 1, 2025, requiring municipalities to separate textiles from general waste. The collection infrastructure is already being built.

If you're manufacturing, importing, or selling textile and footwear products; either or through traditional retail or online channels here's what's coming:

Financial obligations. You'll need to budget for EPR fees. These will vary by product based on eco-modulation (durability, repairability, recyclability).

Design decisions just got expensive (or profitable). Your material choices and design for end-of-life now have direct cost implications. Design for circularity, and you'll pay less. Design for disposability, and you'll pay more.

Contractual reviews. Retailers and brand owners may face cost pass-throughs from suppliers. You need to clarify who bears EPR costs across your supply chain.

E-commerce platforms, you're included. Online marketplaces will need to monitor compliance by third-party sellers to avoid potential liability.

Integration with Digital Product Passports. The introduction of EPR for textiles is expected to work hand-in-hand with the Digital Product Passport under the EU Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation.

Some countries didn't wait for the EU mandate. France was the pioneer in implementing EPR for textiles, introducing the regulation in 2008. In the Netherlands, a new law took effect in July 2023, requiring producers to pay a fee for textiles introduced to the Dutch market.

The infrastructure takes time to build, but it does work. France has demonstrated that EPR can fund collection and sorting systems that keep textiles out of landfill and back into circulation.

If you snooze, you loose.

Don't wait until 2028 to start thinking about this. Companies should begin preparing now, including mapping product portfolios to understand which SKUs fall within scope, reviewing contracts across the supply chain to clarify who will bear EPR costs, and engaging with national authorities and industry bodies to anticipate fee structures and reporting obligations.

>>\> Here's my recommendation based on years working in circular economy and climate coaching:

Audit your product portfolio now. Which products will incur the highest EPR fees? Can you redesign them?

Review your supply chain contracts. Who's responsible for EPR costs? Get clarity before fees start applying.

Design for circularity. This isn't just about compliance, it's about competitive advantage. Early investment in durable, recyclable design will position you ahead of competitors still selling disposable products.

Engage with Producer Responsibility Organisations (PROs). In practice, textile producers will fulfil their obligations by joining or funding Producer Responsibility Organisations, collective schemes that manage waste collection and recycling on behalf of producers.

Monitor national implementations. Each member state will transpose the directive differently. Track developments in your key markets.

### From Voluntary to Mandatory

For decades, sustainable design has been positioned as optional, a matter of corporate social responsibility or brand values. This regulation changes that fundamentally. About time!

EPR makes circular design a financial imperative. It shifts textile waste from being society's problem to being the producer's responsibility.

Nature doesn't produce waste: about time we learned from 3.8 billion years of proven, refined mechanisms. The businesses that see this as an opportunity rather than just compliance will be the ones that thrive in a circular economy.
