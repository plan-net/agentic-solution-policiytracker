# Hungary’s Supreme Court decision confirms limited liability of platform service providers under the Digital Services Act

{DB8D8118-594F-4A86-885B-B52D72DB75FA}

Regional Capabilities

A recent judgment of the Hungarian Supreme Court includes important findings in relation to the liability of online platform service providers. The Court held that when assessing consumer protection liability of an online platform that acts as a hosting service provider, the E-Commerce Act and the Digital Services Act (DSA)—which provide for a specific exemption for hosting service providers—should apply.

More precisely, under the DSA and E-Commerce Act, a hosting service provider is not liable for information stored at the request of a user if it has no actual knowledge of the unlawful activity or unlawful content and, once it becomes aware of such storage, takes steps to remove or block access to the unlawful content. In this respect, the Supreme Court recalled that the recitals of the DSA explicitly refer to the liability of hosting service providers and its limitation, stressing that the DSA cannot provide for any new form of liability, only for cases of exemption, and that these rules apply to any type of liability.

This case specifically addresses the distribution of products by independent sellers through marketplace platforms. The core issue is that products that were not allowed to be distributed in Hungary were made available on the marketplace—not by the platform operator itself, but by third-party sellers. As long as the products are not distributed by the platform operator, the principle of the platform as a hosting service keeps intact the provider’s limited liability.

The Supreme Court stated that the DSA “is also applicable to consumer protection relationships, since it concerns electronic (digital) commerce, a form of trade where the rules on unfair commercial practices towards consumers must also apply, given the increasing expansion of internet commerce.” The horizontal nature of these rules, i.e. the fact that they also cover administrative law, is also confirmed by the E-Commerce Act. In addition, the Supreme Court pointed out that the DSA lays down the requirement for the concept of “unlawful content” to have a broad interpretation. It also identified as falling within the scope of the DSA infringements of consumer protection legislation, including the sale of non-compliant products—i.e. the hosting service provider may be exempted in such cases.

The Supreme Court also confirmed that the liability of an undertaking operating an online platform is not objective with regard to the unlawful content appearing on its website, and that in order to establish liability it is also necessary for it to have actual knowledge of the unlawful content or activity or, once it becomes aware, it fails to take immediate action to remedy the unlawful situation. It follows that hosting service providers do not have a general obligation to monitor or supervise.

Ultimately, the decision establishes as a matter of principle that when assessing the consumer protection liability of hosting service providers operating online platforms, authorities must consider both the provisions of the Act on the Prohibition of Unfair Business-to-Consumer Commercial Practices and the Consumer Protection Act as well as laws governing exemptions from liability.

This judgment clarifies the legal position of platform service providers in Hungary and forms a domestic practice in line with European Union regulation. As a consequence of the decision, it has become clear to platform service providers that their activities fall under the scope of the DSA, which may provide them with an exemption, under specific conditions, from certain infringements conducted by their partners using their platform services.
