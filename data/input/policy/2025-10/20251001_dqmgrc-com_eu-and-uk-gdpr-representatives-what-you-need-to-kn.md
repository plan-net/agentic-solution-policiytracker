EU and UK GDPR Representatives: What You Need to Know

Since the GDPR took effect in 2018, and with Brexit creating a separate UK GDPR regime in 2021, many organisations outside Europe now face extra compliance duties.

If your business processes the personal data of EU or UK residents but has no presence in those regions, you may be legally required to appoint a data protection representative.

Failing to do so is not a minor oversight. Supervisory authorities can impose fines of up to €10 million under the EU GDPR or £8.7 million under the UK GDPR, along with other enforcement measures. Beyond the financial risk, non-compliance undermines customer trust and damages business credibility.

## What is a GDPR representative?

A GDPR representative is a natural or legal person appointed to act as the local contact for organisations that are not established in the EU or UK but process the personal data of people there.

- They are distinct from a DPO (data protection officer). A DPO oversees compliance within an organisation, whereas a representative acts as the external point of contact for regulators and individuals.
- The legal requirement comes from Article 27 of the EU GDPR and the UK GDPR. Both require non-resident organisations to designate a representative if their processing falls within scope.

## Who needs an EU or UK GDPR representative?

You are likely to need one if your organisation:

- Has no branch, office or subsidiary in the EU or UK, and
- Offers goods or services to people in those regions, or
- Monitors or profiles individuals’ behaviour (e.g. through cookies, analytics, or apps).
- If you process data from both EU and UK residents, you may need to appoint two representatives – one in each jurisdiction. Having one does not satisfy the requirements of the other.

Typical businesses caught by this rule include:

- SaaS providers offering Cloud services globally.
- E-commerce retailers shipping goods to EU or UK customers.
- B2B service providers with clients in Europe.

Exemptions exist, but they are narrow. You do not need a representative if you are a public authority or if your processing is truly occasional, does not involve large-scale sensitive data and poses little risk to individuals’ rights.

## What does a GDPR representative do?

A representative’s main responsibilities include:

- Acting as the first point of contact for data protection authorities (such as the ICO (Information Commissioner’s Office) in the UK or national DPAs (data protection authorities) in the EU).
- Handling DSARs (data subject access requests) and other queries from individuals.
- Maintaining records of processing activities, as required under Article 30.
- Relaying communications between your organisation and local regulators.

Practical example: if a Spanish regulator wants to investigate how your US-based SaaS platform handles EU data, it will contact your EU representative directly. Similarly, if a UK customer requests access to their data, your UK representative must respond on your behalf.

## What happens if you don’t appoint one?

Non-compliance with Article 27 can lead to:

- **Financial penalties** Up to €10 million or 2% of global turnover in the EU; up to £8.7 million or 2% of turnover in the UK – whichever is greater.
- **Regulatory action** Supervisory authorities may order you to stop processing personal data in their jurisdiction.
- **Reputational damage** Customers and partners are less likely to trust an organisation that fails to meet basic legal obligations.

The risk is not hypothetical – regulators have already warned and penalised non-compliant organisations for failing to meet representative obligations.

## How to choose the right GDPR representative

When appointing a representative, look for:

- **Expertise** Strong knowledge of EU and/or UK data protection law.
- **Availability** Able to respond quickly to regulators and individuals.
- **Reputation** Transparent, reliable, with a track record in compliance.
- **Capacity** Able to handle data subject requests and maintain accurate records.

Some organisations consider whether to appoint a local solicitor, but in practice most choose a specialist GDPR service provider with dedicated experience in data privacy compliance.

## How to appoint a GDPR representative

The process is straightforward but must be properly documented:

1. **Identify the jurisdiction(s)** Determine whether you need an EU representative, a UK representative, or both.
2. **Select your provider** Choose a qualified individual or company based in the relevant region.
3. **Formalise the appointment** Put the agreement in writing, outlining the representative’s role, responsibilities and authority.
4. **Maintain compliance** Ensure your representative has access to up-to-date records of processing activities and is able to act promptly on your behalf.

Best practice is to treat your representative as an integral part of your data protection framework rather than a tick-box exercise.
